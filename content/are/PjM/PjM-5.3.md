## What this objective tests
Can you run the processes that make the disciplines' documents agree with each other and make the design buildable? That covers model coordination and clash detection, background exchanges, drawing–specification cross-checks, existing-conditions verification, and constructability reviews, often with the CM. It's A/E: decide what to coordinate, in what order, and how.

## Coordination processes
- **A coordination schedule:**
  - regular background exchanges at set dates (with change logs);
  - "freeze" dates before milestones;
  - coordination meetings with an **issue log** (who, what, by when).
- **BIM coordination:**
  - combine the discipline models and run clash detection at set intervals, not just before submission;
  - **prioritize clashes** with fixed or hard-to-move elements (structure, shafts, cores, sloped piping) and anything on the critical path;
  - soft clashes (sprinkler heads against light fixtures) are many but easy to fix.
- **Drawings against specifications:** cross-check keynotes and spec sections. Everything specified should be shown, everything shown should be specified, and terms should be consistent. Under A201-2017 §1.2.1 the documents are **complementary**. Write them so they never need a tie-breaker.
- **Ceiling and plenum checks:** floor-to-floor − structure − required ceiling height − ceiling and fixture depth = space left for ducts, pipes and sprinklers. Do this early. It drives floor-to-floor heights.
- **Fire-rated assemblies:** a life-safety plan shows the rated walls. Overlay it with the MEP drawings to locate dampers and firestopping at penetrations.

## Typical architectural ↔ other-discipline coordination items
- Structural:
  - slab edges and curtain-wall attachment;
  - openings and shafts;
  - roof equipment supports and screens;
  - depressed slabs.
- MEP: ceiling heights and plenum space, shafts, equipment rooms and clearances, diffuser and fixture layout on the reflected ceiling plan.
- Civil and landscape: finish floor elevations, entries, accessible routes, utility entries, drainage at the building line.

## Constructability
- Review for:
  - access and sequence (how large equipment gets in);
  - tolerances between trades;
  - lead times and availability of materials;
  - site logistics;
  - details that can actually be built in the field.
- **Renovations:** check existing conditions with surveys, probes and selective demolition investigations, and the owner's hazardous-materials survey. Don't rely only on the old drawings.
- **With a CMc:** use the CM's constructability and pricing input during DD and CDs. Evaluate its proposals (e.g., precast instead of cast-in-place) against the design intent, cost and schedule, with the consultant concerned. Then take the decision to the owner.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Resolving the clash group with the **most** clashes first. Structure and other fixed elements come first.

> [!TRAP] Letting consultants work from outdated backgrounds. Exchange backgrounds on a schedule, with change logs.

> [!TRAP] Assuming means and methods are part of a constructability review. Constructability means the **design** can be built. How it's built stays with the contractor.
