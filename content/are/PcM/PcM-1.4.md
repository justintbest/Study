## What this objective tests
What does the law expect of an architect? Not perfection, but **reasonable professional care**. This objective covers:
- what happens when there are errors or omissions;
- how to respond when conditions are unknown;
- the newer piece: the duty to address **climate-related vulnerabilities and risks** appropriately.

## Key concepts

### The standard of care
- **Definition (paraphrased):** perform services with the skill and care ordinarily provided by architects practicing in the same or similar locality under the same or similar circumstances.
  - B101-2017 §2.2 puts this into the contract.
  - B101 §2.2 also requires the architect to perform as quickly as is consistent with that skill and care and the orderly progress of the project.
- It's a **negligence** standard. To win, a claimant must prove:
  1. **duty**;
  2. **breach** (falling below the standard of care);
  3. **causation**;
  4. **damages**.
- The standard is usually **established by expert testimony** from other architects. A jury doesn't decide it on its own instincts.
- **Errors happen.** An imperfect set of documents is not automatically negligent. The question is whether a reasonably careful architect would have made the same mistake.

### Contract language that raises the bar
Words like *guarantee, warrant, ensure, certify, highest, best,* or *all* can create an **express warranty**. That is a higher standard than negligence and is generally **not insurable** under professional liability policies.
- Strike or revise them. For example: "certify" → "state that, to the best of the architect's knowledge, information and belief…"
- B101-2017 §10.4: the architect isn't required to sign certifications beyond the scope of its services.

### Errors vs. omissions, and betterment
- **Error:** something shown or specified incorrectly (wrong beam size, conflicting dimensions).
- **Omission:** something that should have been included but wasn't (missing flashing, missing fire damper).
- **Betterment:** if an omitted item has to be added during construction, the owner usually pays what it would have cost in the original bid. The owner would have paid for it anyway. The architect may be responsible only for the **added** cost caused by the late addition, such as demolition, rework, delay and premium pricing.
- **Owner's contingency:** owners should carry a contingency for unforeseen conditions and for the reality that documents are never perfect. Recommending one is good practice.

### Unknown and hidden conditions
- **Existing buildings:** the architect should recommend appropriate investigation, such as surveys, testing, and selective demolition or probes.
  - Under B101-2017 Art. 5, the **owner** furnishes surveys, geotechnical information, and tests or reports such as hazardous-material investigations.
  - The architect may rely on the information the owner provides.
- **Hazardous materials:** B101-2017 §10.6 says the architect isn't responsible for discovering, handling, removing or disposing of hazardous materials, or for people's exposure to them, at the site.
  - If you suspect them (e.g., pipe insulation in a 1960s building), tell the owner and recommend testing by a qualified consultant.
  - Don't ignore them, and don't take over abatement.
- **Discovery during construction:** document it, notify the owner, and evaluate it. Resolve it through the contract's change process (RFI, then a change order or construction change directive). Extra design work is usually an **additional service**.

### Climate vulnerabilities and risk
The current objective explicitly includes the responsibility to address climate-change vulnerabilities and risks *appropriately*. In practice:
- Identify **reasonably foreseeable** climate-related hazards for the site and building type: flooding and sea-level rise, extreme heat, wildfire, high wind and storms, drought.
- **Advise the client** about vulnerabilities and resilience options and their cost and benefit, including options that go **beyond code minimums**. Document the client's decisions.
- **Codes are a minimum.** They reflect past data. As climate risk information becomes widely available, what a "reasonably careful architect" does may change too.
- Don't overpromise. Recommend, explain, and document. Don't guarantee resilience outcomes.

## Rules of thumb
- **"Ordinary, reasonable, prudent"**, not "perfect" or "best."
- Your **duty to protect public health, safety and welfare** exists regardless of contract terms. You can't contract out of code compliance.
- When something unexpected shows up: **stop → notify → investigate → document → resolve through the contract**.
- Use clear, qualified language in reports. State the basis and the limits of your observations.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Choosing "the architect guarantees…" or "the architect will ensure…" answers. They describe warranties above the standard of care.

> [!TRAP] Assuming the architect pays the whole cost of an omitted item. Betterment means the owner pays what the item would have cost originally.

> [!TRAP] Treating code compliance as the full extent of the architect's duty. Codes are minimums. Where foreseeable risks such as climate hazards are known, the architect should advise the client.

> [!TRAP] Letting the architect test or remove suspected hazardous materials. Notify the owner and recommend a qualified consultant.
