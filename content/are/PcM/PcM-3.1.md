## What this objective tests
**Before** a contract is signed, the architect must assess:
- what services the client actually needs;
- whether the request is complete;
- whether there are public-interest or other conflicts;
- what a sound fee proposal looks like.

It's an A/E objective: weigh several factors and choose the best response to a request for proposal or a client inquiry.

## Key concepts

### Go / no-go: should we pursue this?
Weigh these factors:
- **Fit:** the firm's expertise, building type and strategic goals.
- **Capacity:** staff available when the work arrives (see 1.1).
- **Client:** financial capability, decision-making clarity, history with consultants, and whether funding is secured.
- **Risk:** project type, contract terms, schedule and budget realism (see 3.3).
- **Competition and cost to pursue:** proposal and interview effort versus the odds of winning.
- **Profit potential and strategic value:** a new market, a repeat client, reputation.

Declining is a legitimate answer. A well-run firm says no to poor-fit or high-risk work.

### Types of requests
- **RFQ (request for qualifications):** experience, team and approach. Usually no fee.
- **RFP (request for proposal):** scope understanding, approach, schedule, and often a fee.
- **Qualifications-based selection (QBS):** the owner picks the most qualified firm first, *then* negotiates a fair and reasonable fee. If they can't agree, the owner moves to the next-ranked firm.
  - The **Brooks Act** requires QBS for federal A/E contracts (40 U.S.C. §§1101–1104).
  - Many states and localities have similar laws for public work.
- **Sole-source / direct negotiation:** typical with repeat private clients.

### Assessing the request's completeness
Before proposing, check that you have, or can define:
- the program, or whether programming is a service you'll need to provide;
- the budget, including what it covers (construction only, or the total project cost);
- the schedule and key milestones;
- site information: surveys, geotechnical data, existing drawings;
- the delivery method and the owner's other consultants;
- who has decision-making authority;
- any regulatory approvals expected: zoning variances, historic review, environmental review.

**Missing items** → ask questions in writing (most RFPs allow them), state your **assumptions** in the proposal, or include the missing work as **services** (programming, existing-conditions survey).

**Spot mismatches between budget, program and schedule.** A program too large for the budget is a risk to flag *now*, not after schematic design.

### Public-interest conflicts
- **Personal or financial conflicts:** an architect serving on a public board that reviews the project; a relative on the selection committee; ownership in the project or a supplier. **Disclose and recuse** (see 1.3).
- **Projects that conflict with the public interest or the law:** an owner asking you to help evade zoning or accessibility requirements, or to misrepresent the project to an agency. Decline.
- **Two clients competing for the same site or the same approvals:** disclose to both and get consent, or decline one.

### Building the fee proposal
1. **Define the scope:** basic services, supplemental services and exclusions (B101-2017 Arts. 3 and 4).
2. **Bottom-up estimate:** a staffing plan by phase and task (hours × rates), plus consultants (with markup), reimbursables and contingency.
3. **Top-down check:** compare with a percentage of construction cost, historical data from similar projects, or published fee curves.
4. **Reconcile** the two, then decide on the fee method (see 2.3) and a payment schedule.
5. **State the assumptions** (deliverables, meetings, number of reviews, schedule) so changes can be recognized later.

### Example phase split (traditional; adjust to the project)
| Phase | Share of basic fee |
|---|---|
| Schematic design | ~15% |
| Design development | ~20% |
| Construction documents | ~40% |
| Procurement (bidding/negotiation) | ~5% |
| Construction administration | ~20% |

> [!VERIFY] The phase percentages are a widely taught rule of thumb. B101-2017 §11.5 leaves them blank for the parties to fill in. Use them as a sanity check, not a rule.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Submitting a fee before you understand the scope. When the request is incomplete, the best answer clarifies the scope first.

> [!TRAP] Assuming QBS means the cheapest qualified firm wins. Under QBS, fee is negotiated *after* selection on qualifications.

> [!TRAP] Accepting a project with an obviously unrealistic budget-to-program ratio and staying silent. Raise it before contracting.

> [!TRAP] Never declining work. Turning down poor-fit or high-risk work is often the A/E answer.
