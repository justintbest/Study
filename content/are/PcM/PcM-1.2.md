## What this objective tests
Running a firm means complying with employment law and carrying the right insurance. Expect questions like:
- Is this person an employee or an independent contractor?
- Does this worker get overtime?
- Which policy responds to this loss?
- What happens to coverage when the firm switches carriers?

It's a U/A objective: know the categories and apply them.

## Key concepts

### Employees vs. independent contractors
- The label in the contract doesn't decide it. **Control** does. Who sets hours and methods, supplies the tools, and directs the work? Is the person economically independent with other clients?
- Typical tests: the IRS common-law factors (behavioral control, financial control, type of relationship) and the U.S. Department of Labor's *economic reality* test.
- **Misclassification risk:** back payroll taxes, unpaid overtime, benefits, penalties.
- Work an independent contractor creates is **not automatically owned by the firm**, so get a written assignment of copyright (see 1.3, 2.3).

### Wage and hour (FLSA)
- **Non-exempt** employees get **1.5× their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek**.
- **Exempt** status (e.g., the *learned professional* exemption) needs *both* a qualifying job duties test *and* payment on a salary basis above the federal threshold. A job title alone never makes someone exempt.
- Interns at for-profit firms generally must be paid. Unpaid arrangements are allowed only in narrow cases under DOL's "primary beneficiary" test.
- States may set higher minimum wages and stricter overtime rules. When both apply, the stricter rule controls.

### Anti-discrimination and leave laws (federal thresholds)
| Law | Covers | Employer size |
|---|---|---|
| Title VII, Civil Rights Act | Race, color, religion, sex, national origin | 15+ employees |
| ADA Title I | Disability; reasonable accommodation | 15+ employees |
| ADEA | Age 40 and over | 20+ employees |
| FMLA | Up to 12 weeks unpaid, job-protected leave | 50+ employees (eligibility rules apply) |
| COBRA | Continuation of group health coverage | 20+ employees |
| ACA employer mandate | Offer affordable coverage | 50+ full-time equivalents |
| Equal Pay Act | Equal pay for equal work regardless of sex | Essentially all FLSA-covered employers |

> [!VERIFY] The thresholds above are the long-standing federal numbers. Check current DOL/EEOC guidance, and note that state laws often cover smaller employers.

### OSHA
- Employers must provide a workplace free of recognized hazards (the *General Duty Clause*).
- For architects, the big exposure is **staff visiting construction sites**: personal protective equipment, training, and following the contractor's site safety program.
- The **contractor** remains responsible for jobsite safety and construction means and methods (A201-2017 §3.3.1, §10). Architects shouldn't take on site-safety control by contract or by conduct.

### Insurance a practice carries
| Policy | Responds to | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Professional liability (E&O)** | Claims alleging negligent acts, errors or omissions in professional services | Almost always **claims-made**. Defense costs may erode the limit. A deductible applies per claim |
| **Commercial general liability (CGL)** | Bodily injury or property damage from the firm's non-professional operations (e.g., a visitor slips in the office) | Usually **occurrence** based. **Excludes professional services** |
| **Workers' compensation** | Employee work-related injury or illness, regardless of fault | Mandated by state law. Generally the employee's *exclusive remedy* against the employer |
| **Employer's liability** | Employee injury claims outside workers' comp | Usually bundled with workers' comp |
| **Auto: owned, hired, non-owned** | Vehicles used for business, including employees' own cars on firm business | Non-owned auto coverage is commonly overlooked |
| **Umbrella / excess** | Adds limits above CGL, auto and employer's liability | Usually **not** above professional liability |
| **Property / business owner's policy (BOP), valuable papers** | Office contents, records, business interruption | |
| **Employment practices liability (EPLI)** | Discrimination, harassment, wrongful termination claims | |
| **Cyber liability** | Data breach, ransomware, funds-transfer fraud | |
| **Health, disability, key-person life** | Employee benefits; continuity | Health coverage obligations depend on firm size |

### Claims-made vs. occurrence
- **Claims-made** (professional liability): the policy in force **when the claim is made** responds, as long as the act happened after the policy's **retroactive date**.
- When you switch carriers, keep the original retro date (*prior acts* coverage).
- When you close or retire, buy an **extended reporting period** ("tail").
- Report potential claims (known circumstances) promptly, or coverage can be denied.
- **Occurrence** (most CGL): the policy in force **when the injury happened** responds, even if the claim comes years later.

## Rules of thumb
- Professional mistake → professional liability. Physical injury or damage from office operations → CGL. Employee hurt → workers' comp.
- Never admit fault or settle a claim without the insurer's consent. Notify the carrier first.
- Contract insurance requirements (e.g., B101-2017 §2.5) should match what the firm actually carries. Check before signing.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Assuming CGL covers a design error. It excludes professional services. A design error claim goes to professional liability.

> [!TRAP] Letting a claims-made policy lapse at retirement. Without tail coverage, a claim made later is uninsured, even though the policy was in force when the work was done.

> [!TRAP] Calling someone an "independent contractor" because the contract says so. Control and economic dependence decide classification.

> [!TRAP] Thinking a salaried employee is automatically exempt. Exemption needs the duties test and the salary test.
