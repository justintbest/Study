## What this objective tests
Business decisions in action:
- How do you **negotiate services and fees**?
- How do you **control scope creep**?
- How do you **adjust scope** without giving away fee?
- How do you **protect intellectual property**, including digital files?

It's a U/A objective. Know the right move and its consequences.

## Negotiating services and contracts
- Negotiate **scope first, fee second**. A fee only means something against a defined scope.
- **Trade scope for fee rather than cutting fee alone.** If the client's budget is fixed, remove or phase services, for example making construction administration hourly or deleting renderings. Don't just discount.
- Understand the client's interests (schedule, cost certainty, quality, risk tolerance), and know your own walk-away point. In negotiation-theory terms, that's your BATNA: your best alternative to a negotiated agreement.
- Put everything in writing. Use the **initial information** (B101-2017 Art. 1) to record the assumptions behind the fee: program, budget, schedule, site, and the delivery method.

### Fee methods
| Method | Best when | Risk to architect |
|---|---|---|
| **Stipulated sum (lump sum)** | Scope is well defined | Overruns come out of profit |
| **Percentage of construction cost** | Traditional, simple | Fee rises with cost, which can look like a conflict of interest; fee falls if cost is cut |
| **Hourly / time and materials** | Scope unclear (studies, CA, existing conditions) | Low for the architect; the client lacks cost certainty |
| **Hourly not-to-exceed** | The client wants a cap on unclear work | The architect absorbs overruns beyond the cap |
| **Multiple of direct personnel expense / direct salary expense** | Like hourly, tied to the firm's costs | Requires transparent payroll records |
| **Unit cost (per sf, per unit, per key)** | Repetitive project types | Unusual conditions erode the fee |
| **Value-based / royalty** | Architect's work creates measurable client value | Needs strong negotiating position |

Common hybrids: a stipulated sum for design phases + hourly for CA, or a stipulated sum + hourly for additional services.

## Scope creep vs. scope adjustment
- **Scope creep** is the gradual, *unacknowledged* growth of services. The client asks for "just one more option," a new program element, or extra meetings.
- **Fix it at the first instance:**
  1. recognize the request is outside scope;
  2. tell the client in writing;
  3. state the fee and schedule impact;
  4. get **written authorization before proceeding**.

  Under B101-2017 §4.2, some additional services need prior written authorization. For others, the architect notifies the owner and the owner can tell the architect if the services aren't wanted. Read §4.2.2–4.2.3 for the specific lists.
- **Basic service limits.** B101-2017 lets you set the number of submittal reviews, site visits, and inspections for substantial and final completion (§4.2.4). Work beyond those limits is an additional service.
- **Material change to the project.** If the scope of the project or the services changes materially, compensation is equitably adjusted (B101-2017 §11.6).
- **Owner's budget changes.** The owner shouldn't significantly increase or decrease the budget, or the project's scope and quality, without the architect agreeing to a corresponding change in scope and compensation (B101-2017 Art. 5).
- **Don't let the project team give away services.** Train project managers to recognize scope changes and route them through the firm's change process.

## Protecting intellectual property
- **Copyright stays with the architect.** The owner gets a nonexclusive license to use the instruments of service for *this* project (B101-2017 Art. 7). The license is conditioned on the owner meeting its obligations, including payment.
  - If the owner terminates the agreement for convenience, the license may require a **licensing fee** (B101-2017 §11.9).
- **Digital data and BIM:**
  - agree on transmission protocols, permitted uses and reliance up front, using AIA **E203** (BIM and Digital Data Exhibit) with the **G201/G202** protocol forms;
  - use **C106** (Digital Data Licensing Agreement) when sharing files with parties you have no other agreement with;
  - add disclaimers that the electronic files are not the contract documents; the sealed set governs.
- **Releasing native files to contractors:** do it under a license or release that limits use to the project and disclaims accuracy. Include indemnity if the files are modified.
- **Register important designs** with the U.S. Copyright Office if you might need to enforce them (see 1.3).
- **Other IP:**
  - trademarks for the firm's name and logo;
  - **trade secrets** such as proprietary details, client lists and fee data, protected with confidentiality agreements;
  - **non-solicitation or confidentiality** clauses in employment agreements, where enforceable.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Doing the extra work first and asking for payment later. The best answer is notify and get written authorization *before* proceeding.

> [!TRAP] Cutting the fee to meet a client's budget without cutting scope. Adjust services to match the fee.

> [!TRAP] Giving the owner ownership of the copyright because the owner paid. The owner gets a license, not ownership, unless the agreement says otherwise.

> [!TRAP] Handing over native CAD or BIM files with no agreement. Use a digital data license or protocol.

> [!VERIFY] AIA document numbers E203-2013, G201-2013, G202-2013 and C106-2013 are from my knowledge and aren't on NCARB's ARE contract list. Confirm current editions on aiacontracts.com.
