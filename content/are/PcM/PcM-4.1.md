## What this objective tests
How should a practice be **legally structured** and **organized** to reach its goals? It's an A/E objective. Compare the options on:
- liability
- taxation
- ownership and transfer
- governance
- fit with the firm's strategy

## Legal business structures
| Structure | Owner liability for business debts | Taxation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sole proprietorship** | **Unlimited, personal** | Pass-through (owner's personal return) | Simplest; no separation between owner and business; ends with the owner |
| **General partnership** | **Unlimited, joint and several:** each partner is liable for the partnership's debts, including other partners' acts | Pass-through | A partnership agreement is critical: capital, profit split, buy-sell, dissolution |
| **Limited liability partnership (LLP)** | Partners are generally shielded from *other* partners' malpractice and partnership debts (extent varies by state) | Pass-through | Common for professional firms where state law allows |
| **Limited liability company (LLC / PLLC)** | Members are generally shielded from business debts | Pass-through by default (can elect corporate taxation) | Flexible management and profit allocation; a "professional" LLC (PLLC) may be required for licensed professions in some states |
| **C corporation (incl. professional corporation, PC)** | Shareholders are generally shielded | **Double taxation:** the corporation pays tax on profits, then shareholders pay tax on dividends | Easy to add owners and transfer stock; formal governance (board, officers, minutes) |
| **S corporation** | Shareholders are generally shielded | Pass-through | Federal limits: no more than 100 shareholders, one class of stock, individual (U.S.) shareholders generally |

### The rule that never changes
**No entity shields a professional from liability for their own negligence.** An LLC or corporation protects owners from the *business's* debts and from *other* owners' acts, as state law provides. It does **not** protect the architect who personally made the negligent error. That's why professional liability insurance is still essential.

### Licensing overlay
Most jurisdictions regulate **firms**, not just individuals. Common requirements include:
- a firm registration or certificate of authorization;
- a licensed architect in **responsible control** of the architectural work;
- rules on who may own or direct a firm offering architecture (some jurisdictions require a minimum share of licensed owners or directors);
- rules on firm names.

These vary by jurisdiction. Always check the board's rules before forming or expanding into a new state.

> [!VERIFY] Ownership and firm-registration rules are jurisdiction-specific. NCARB's Model Law has provisions, but boards adopt their own versions. The ARE tests the *concept* that firms must meet board requirements.

## Organizational structures (how the work is organized)
| Model | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Departmental (functional)** | Work passes between design, production and CA departments | Deep specialization; efficient production | Hand-offs lose knowledge; weaker client continuity |
| **Studio / team** | A stable team carries a project from start to finish | Continuity, accountability, client relationships | Uneven workload across studios |
| **Matrix** | Staff report to both project leaders and discipline or market leaders | Shares expertise across projects | Two bosses; potential conflicts |
| **Market sectors** | Studios organized by building type (healthcare, education) | Deep market expertise and marketing focus | Exposure if one market slows |

Firm positioning: some practices lead with **ideas** (design-driven), some with **service** (client relationship), some with **delivery** (efficient, repeatable). Structure should support the chosen position.

## Growth, partnering and transition
- **Joint venture (JV):** a separate legal relationship for a specific project. JV partners are typically **jointly and severally liable** to the client. The JV needs its own agreement (AIA C101) and insurance that covers JV work.
- **Prime / sub (association):** one firm holds the owner contract and the other is its consultant. Liability flows through the prime.
- **Mergers and acquisitions:** due diligence on liabilities, open claims, insurance (tail coverage for the acquired firm), culture and staff retention.
- **Ownership transition:**
  - internal sale to key employees, often over time;
  - an **employee stock ownership plan (ESOP)**;
  - external sale.
  - A **buy-sell agreement** sets how and at what price an owner's interest is bought on death, disability, retirement or departure. **Key-person life or disability insurance** can fund it.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Thinking an LLC or corporation protects the architect from their *own* malpractice. It doesn't.

> [!TRAP] Recommending a general partnership to limit liability. General partners have unlimited, joint and several liability.

> [!TRAP] Forgetting double taxation for C corporations. If the scenario emphasizes avoiding entity-level tax, think S corp, LLC or partnership.

> [!TRAP] Treating a joint venture as low risk. Each venturer may be liable for the whole JV's obligations to the client.

> [!VERIFY] S-corporation limits and the C-corporation tax rate are federal tax law, which changes. The ARE won't test specific tax rates.
