## What this objective tests
Given a situation, what's the ethical and lawful response? Expect scenarios involving:
- plagiarism, copyright or intellectual-property infringement
- exaggerating qualifications or credit
- defamation (libel or slander) of competitors
- conflicts of interest, gifts
- client requests that cross a line

The ARE expects you to know **which rulebook applies** and **what the conduct rules require**.

## Three layers of rules
| Source | Who it binds | Enforcement |
|---|---|---|
| **State licensing law and board rules** | Every licensed architect in that jurisdiction | License discipline (fine, suspension, revocation) |
| **NCARB Model Rules of Conduct** | Nobody directly. It's a model that boards adopt in whole or in part | Through each board's adopted rules |
| **AIA Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** (2024 edition on the ARE) | **AIA members only** | AIA National Ethics Council (admonition, censure, suspension, termination of membership). Not license action |

The AIA Code is organized into **Canons** (broad principles), **Ethical Standards** (goals) and **Rules of Conduct** (mandatory). There are six canons:
1. General Obligations
2. Obligations to the Public
3. Obligations to the Client
4. Obligations to the Profession
5. Obligations to Colleagues
6. Obligations to the Environment

## Core conduct principles (paraphrased)
- **Competence.** Take on only work you, with your consultants, are qualified to perform.
- **Law.** Don't knowingly violate the law or help a client do something you know is illegal or fraudulent.
- **Public safety over client wishes.** If a client or employer overrules you on something that violates building laws and materially endangers public safety, the NCARB model approach is to:
  1. refuse to consent;
  2. report it to the building official;
  3. withdraw from the project if the conduct continues.

  The AIA Code similarly requires advising the client and taking appropriate action.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Disclose any financial interest or business relationship that could affect your judgment. Don't take compensation from more than one party on a project without written disclosure and agreement. Never accept payment from suppliers for specifying their products.
- **Gifts to public officials.** Never offer or give gifts intended to influence an official about a project.
- **Impartiality.** When interpreting the contract documents between owner and contractor, decide impartially.
- **Truthful representation.** Represent your qualifications, and *your actual role* on past projects, accurately. If you worked on a project at a previous firm, say so and give that firm credit.
- **Seal only what you control.** Sign and seal only documents prepared under your responsible control. No "plan stamping."
- **Confidentiality.** Don't disclose client information you've been asked to keep confidential, unless the law or the Code requires it, for example to protect public safety.
- **Credit and departing employees.** Recognize the contributions of employees and colleagues. Departing staff shouldn't take firm designs or documents without permission. Firms shouldn't unreasonably refuse departing staff copies of their own work for portfolios.

## Intellectual property and plagiarism
- **Copyright.** Architects' drawings and the **architectural work itself** (the building design) are protected by copyright as soon as they're fixed in tangible form.
  - The Architectural Works Copyright Protection Act of 1990 added buildings as a protected category: 17 U.S.C. §102(a)(8).
  - **Registration isn't required for protection**, but it **is required before suing** for a U.S. work. Statutory damages and attorneys' fees are generally available only if the work was registered before the infringement, or within three months of publication.
- **Work made for hire.** An employee's work within the scope of employment belongs to the firm. A freelancer's work does **not**, unless there's a written assignment.
- **Not protected:** ideas, general styles, standard features and functional necessities. Photos of a building visible from a public place are allowed (17 U.S.C. §120(a)).
- **Plagiarism vs. infringement.**
  - *Plagiarism* is presenting someone else's work as your own. It's an ethics and honesty problem even when no copyright is violated.
  - *Infringement* is a legal violation of exclusive rights. Copying a competitor's published plans can be both.
- **Using another firm's documents.** Get a license or written permission. Owners usually have only a limited license to use instruments of service for their own project (B101-2017 Art. 7).

## Defamation: libel and slander
- **Libel** = written or published defamation. **Slander** = spoken defamation.
- It requires a **false statement of fact** that harms reputation. **Truth is a defense**; honest opinion based on disclosed facts is generally protected.
- Ethically, architects shouldn't make false or malicious statements about competitors' work. Compete on your own merits.

## Rules of thumb
- If the client wants something **illegal or unsafe**, the answer is never "comply and document."
- If a choice would **misrepresent** anything (credit, qualifications, licensure, cost, schedule), it's wrong.
- When in doubt about a conflict, **disclose in writing and get consent**, or step away.
- "AIA member?" decides whether the AIA Code applies. "Licensed?" decides whether board rules apply.

## Common exam traps
> [!TRAP] Thinking the AIA Code can cost you your license. Only the state licensing board can discipline a license. The AIA can only act on membership.

> [!TRAP] Listing past-firm projects as your own without explanation. You must describe your actual role and credit the firm of record.

> [!TRAP] Assuming no copyright registration means no protection. Protection exists on fixation. Registration affects your ability to sue and the remedies available.

> [!TRAP] Assuming a freelancer's drawings belong to the firm automatically. Without a written assignment, they generally don't.

> [!VERIFY] Rule numbers differ between editions of the AIA Code and the NCARB Model Rules. Learn the principles; confirm specific rule numbers in the current editions.
